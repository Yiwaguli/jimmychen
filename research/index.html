Queering with Fungi

Myceliating queer kinship and belonging through non-representational sensing

PhD research, Creative Computing Institute, University of the Arts London. Ongoing

Queering with Fungi is a practice-based PhD positioned between art, HCI and sound and multisensory practice. It grows mushrooms at home and attends to what happens between queer lives and fungal ones, and to how kinship and belonging can be sensed, grown and shared there.

Myceliate, as a verb

The project uses myceliate as a verb. It imagines a collaborative growth and interconnection between fungi and queer individuals that resembles a mycorrhizal network, the underground web through which fungi connect plants and pass nutrients and signals between them. These networks are among the most crucial parts of a forest, and they stayed almost invisible and unappreciated until recent years.

Fungi and their spores are nearly everywhere: in houses and gardens, in bodies and in daily food. They tend to be noticed only when they stand for something poisonous or mouldy that must be eliminated, a pathogen (Tsing, 2012). Mycophobic language is easily weaponised (Kaishian and Djoulakian, 2020), and mushrooms are caught up in the stranger fetishism and othering that Ahmed (2000) describes. The research detangles and entangles this intersection of queerness and fungal bodies, as a way of cultivating queer-fungal kinship and belonging within East and Southeast Asian (ESEA) queer diasporic communities.

Kaishian and Djoulakian (2020) explore the fungal kingdom from a queer perspective. This research also runs in the other direction and applies a fungal perspective to queerness.

Growing, failing and dying with fungi

The practice began with grow bags kept under a bed. The first bag to be cultivated from scratch was named Jimmy. After forty-one days it fruited and was divided in two, and soon afterwards it was lost to a swarm of gnats. Another, untitled bag grown on fabric put out pale, claw-like forms as it reached for air.

Read alongside Halberstam’s The Queer Art of Failure (2011), these failures of growing became material for the research. They also pointed away from texts, symbols and metaphors and toward practices, movements and affects, the shift that Vannini (2015) describes in non-representational research.

In a performance with a mushroom block called the Volcano, bio-electrical signals detected from the mushrooms set the pitch, tempo and instrumentation of a voice and an improvised keyboard. On stage the mushrooms produced almost nothing, and the piece became a performance of collaborative failure. Not long afterwards the Volcano was overtaken by mould.

Mould is also fungi, growing out of the death of others. Deciding which fungi perform, which are discarded, which are frozen and which are allowed to revive is a small exercise of the sovereignty that Mbembe (2019) analyses in Necropolitics, carried out here in a bedroom and with more-than-human lives. Queering with fungi therefore means more than living, resisting and surviving with them. It also means being disoriented with fungi, failing with fungi and dying with fungi.

At home, in the making

The study is located at home, in the home of a queer diaspora, without being a study of home. Following Ingold (2000), for whom wayfinding is closer to storytelling than to reading a map, and Ahmed’s (2006) account of disorientation, it treats that home as a place with no fixed coordinates. With Hall (1998) on the performativity of home-making, Muñoz (1999) on disidentification and Fortier (2003) on motions of attachment, it argues that there is no home, only the making of home.

Home becomes a continuous negotiation between everyday human activity and non-human agencies. The queer home of a diaspora is always in the making, a place where belonging and kinship are negotiated in motions of more-than-human attachment (McKeithen, 2017).

Layers of practice

The research is built in three intertwined layers, and the plan remains subject to change by the mushrooms.

Practice

Auto-ethnographic study
The growing practice, the multisensory performance practice, and reflection on both.
Participants’ study
Participatory workshops with members of ESEA queer diasporic communities, in which a mushroom grow kit travels home as a cultural probe, a survival kit. Non-textual data analysis and immersive storytelling as archive.
Let the mushrooms speak
Mushroom signals as notation and musical score, the mushrooms with their substrate and environment, and the life of the mushroom as a research subject.
